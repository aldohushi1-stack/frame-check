# Frame Check

Words don't just describe a deal — they edit what people remember and what they choose
(Loftus & Palmer 1974; Loftus & Pickrell 1995; Tversky & Kahneman 1981). This skill uses that
in two directions: to make the user's own words land, honestly, and to stop other people's
framing from landing on them unexamined.

## Pick the mode

- **OUT** — the text is the user's own (a draft, a pitch, a reply, a post, "how do I say…").
- **IN** — the text came from someone else (an ad, offer, quote, contract, email, headline, study claim).
- Unclear → ask once: "Is this yours going out, or theirs coming in?"

## The frame toolkit (name them in both modes)

| Frame | Looks like | Flip it to |
|---|---|---|
| Gain vs loss | "200 saved" / "400 die" | the same fact from the other side |
| Relative vs absolute | "cuts risk by 20%" | "from 5 in 100 to 4 in 100" |
| Small unit | "$124 a week", "less than a coffee a day" | the total over the full term |
| "Up to" / "from" | "save up to $400", "from $99" | what a typical person actually gets |
| Anchor | "was $5,000, now $2,000" | was it ever really sold at $5,000? |
| Urgency / scarcity | "only 3 spots left", "ends Friday" | what happens if you wait? |
| Social proof | "businesses across the country use…" | how many, named, and checkable? |
| Loaded words | "smashed" vs "hit"; "investment" vs "cost" | the plainest neutral word |
| Omission / default | the fine print, the opt-out box | what's missing from the headline |
| Authority | "studies show", "experts say" | which study, how many people, who paid? |

## The honesty gate (every OUT frame must pass all four)

1. **True?** The frame describes the real facts, not a nicer fiction.
2. **Survives daylight?** It would still work if the reader knew exactly why it was worded that way.
3. **Serves them too?** The reader genuinely benefits from seeing it this way.
4. **Leaves the past alone?** Never reframe what already happened — a promise, a complaint, a deal, a
   conversation. That is the Loftus effect, and it is where trust breaks.

Any frame that fails a question is shown as **REJECTED** with the reason, not quietly dropped — so
the user sees the line they are choosing not to cross.

## OUT procedure

1. **Core facts.** List the plain, true facts the message rests on (price, what they get, proof). If a
   fact is missing, ask for it or leave a clearly marked placeholder. Never invent numbers, clients,
   results or social proof.
2. **Current frame.** Name the frame the draft is using now, and what it makes the reader feel.
3. **Rewrites.** Give 2–3 versions, each built on a different frame, labelled
   (e.g. "Loss frame", "Small-unit frame", "Specific-proof frame"), so the user can compare.
4. **Gate table.** Run each version through the four questions; mark PASS or REJECTED.
5. **The ask.** End each version with one clear ask — the single thing the reader should do next.

## IN procedure

1. **The claim.** One line: what they want the user to believe or do.
2. **Frames spotted.** Quote the exact words, and name the frame from the toolkit. Only list a frame
   whose flip could change what a careful reader decides. Plain description, real inclusions and
   ordinary sales tone are not frames — leave them out, or give them one line ("Also noticed: …").
   An honest text may carry none or one; say so plainly rather than straining to fill the table.
3. **Flip it.** Restate the key claim in the opposite frame.
4. **Neutral numbers.** Do the maths in plain terms — total cost over the term, absolute risk,
   typical (not best-case) outcome. Show the working. Mark every assumption (a weekly price × 52 is a
   yearly figure only if the term is a year; ask for the term if it isn't given). If the numbers needed
   are not given, say so rather than guessing.
5. **Questions to ask them.** 3–5 short, specific questions that would expose the rest.
6. **Verdict.** One of: **Fair** (framed, but the flip is fine) · **Pushy but true** (the facts hold;
   the pressure is the frame) · **Misleading** (the flip changes the decision, or key facts are hidden).

## What this is not

- Not a lie detector. A frame is a choice of words, not proof of intent. The verdict is about whether
  the flip changes the reader's decision.
- Not legal or consumer-law advice. It will not say whether a claim breaks a law.
- Not a fact checker. It flags what is missing or unverifiable; it does not verify.

## Output shape

Keep it scannable: short headings, one small table where it helps, no essay. Finish with one line
the user can act on (send version B / ask them question 2 / walk away).
